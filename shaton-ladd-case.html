Chicago Landlord Charged in Shaton Ladd's Death: What We Know

True Dossier · November 22, 2026 · Case: Shaton Ladd

How does a man with a criminal record stretching back nearly four decades end up in a position of trust over other people's homes? That question sits at the center of the Shaton Ladd case in Chicago. The allegations against 58-year-old Jerry Powell are still unproven in court.

Who She Was

Shaton Ladd, 36, was a mother of two young sons. Family and friends knew her as Molly Rico. Photos on her personal Facebook page show her at different moments in her life, and they are the images her relatives now hold onto.

Her family has spoken publicly about what the loss means. "Who wants to live without their mother?" one relative said. Another described her simply as loving. When a reporter asked whether there had been a history of violence or arguments, a relative answered no to both. "She's not a violent person," the relative said.

What Happened

On October 22, 2026, Ladd was found dead inside the apartment where she lived on South Ellis Avenue, in Chicago's Chatham neighborhood on the South Side. The Cook County Medical Examiner's Office determined that she died of strangulation and ruled her death a homicide.

According to prosecutors, Powell strangled Ladd on the living room couch and then covered her body with a white sheet. Prosecutors also allege that around 7 a.m., Ladd's 28-year-old roommate came home from a night shift. They say Powell confronted her with an object that looked like a real gun, threatened her, and sexually assaulted her while Ladd's body was still in the room. Prosecutors allege he used the death as a threat to force her compliance.

The woman managed to quietly reach out to a co-worker for help. That message was passed to the Chicago Police Department.

The Investigation

Around the same time, Powell walked into a Chicago police station on his own. According to prosecutors, he told officers he had killed his girlfriend and gave them the apartment's address. Police and firefighters went there and found Ladd's body on the couch under a white sheet, matching what he had described.

Investigators documented a replica gun and a BB gun at the scene. They still had to determine which object, if either, was used during the alleged threats. The roommate was taken to a hospital for an examination and the collection of forensic evidence.

Prosecutors say Powell acknowledged strangling Ladd but denied sexually assaulting the second woman. He reportedly claimed that he had earlier had consensual sex with both women. Under the law, a past relationship does not equal consent to later sexual activity. His denial would have to be weighed against the survivor's account, the medical findings, and the physical evidence.

News reports described Powell as the landlord of the building. In court, a judge said he lived with both women, though the exact nature of their relationships was unclear.

Court records compiled by the Chicago Sun-Times show a long criminal history. In 1988, Powell received a six-year prison sentence for a serious sexual offense. His record also includes armed robbery, arson, aggravated battery, burglary, and unlawful possession of a weapon. Prior convictions do not prove new allegations, though. Prosecutors must prove the 2026 charges with evidence from the 2026 events.

The Charges and Where the Case Stands

On October 24, 2026, Powell was charged with first-degree murder, aggravated criminal sexual assault involving a weapon, and aggravated kidnapping. A judge ordered him held in custody pending trial. Pretrial detention is a procedural ruling, not a finding of guilt. Powell is presumed innocent unless and until he is convicted.

As of November 6, 2026, no conviction had been confirmed. Ladd's family has expressed anger and called for severe punishment. One relative said, "He deserves every minute, every second, every day." Neighbors say the case has shaken their sense of safety. A memorial was held, and balloons were released into the sky in her memory.

Questions That Remain

Several questions will likely be answered in court or through public records:

  • Supervision: What monitoring requirements, if any, was Powell subject to after his earlier convictions, and when did they end?
  • Warning signs: Were any concerns ever documented while he was under the supervision of authorities?
  • The evidence: How will prosecutors connect the physical and forensic evidence to each separate charge, given that an admission to one act cannot substitute for proof of another?
  • Housing safety: Illinois has registration and supervision mechanisms for people with certain convictions. But they don't apply to everyone for life, and tenants may not know a landlord's history.

None of these questions can be answered by counting prior convictions alone. They require a look at the sentences, the conditions of release, and what authorities actually knew.

Whatever the verdict, two boys will grow up without their mother. Her family says they are still waiting for justice for Shaton Ladd.
